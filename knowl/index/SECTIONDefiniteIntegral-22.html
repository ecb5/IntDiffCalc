Fourier Series.

Mathematicians (and scientists) use the symmetry properties of the sine and cosine function to simplify problems in various ways.
🔗
For example, in the theory of acoustics, sine and cosine waves form the “pure tones” from which all other sound waves are formed. Mathematically, we can think of a sound wave as a function on a closed interval of time (which constitutes one cycle of the sound wave). The “pure tones” are given by different sine and cosine functions of varying frequencies defined on the interval \([-\pi, \pi] \) as in Figure 20.4.0.13. By combining the “pure tones” in different proportions different sounds are created. Conversely any sound wave can be decomposed into a (possibly infinite) sum of “pure tones.” Such a decomposition results in what is called a Fourier Series.
🔗
It can be shown (though we won’t do it here), that if a function \(f(t)\) defined on the interval \([-\pi, \pi]\) can be written as the Fourier Series:
\begin{equation*} a_0+a_1\cos(t)+ b_1 \sin(t) +a_2\cos(2t) + b_2\sin(2t) + a_3\cos(3t) +b_3\sin(3t) + \cdots \end{equation*}
then the coefficients \(a_0, a_1, b_1, a_2, b_2, \cdots \) are given by the definite integrals
\begin{align*} a_0\amp =\frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{t=-\pi }^\pi f(t)\cos(0t) \dx{t}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{t=-\pi}^\pi f(t)\dx{t}\\ a_n\amp =\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi }^\pi f(t)\cos(nt)\dx{t}, \text{ if } n\ge 1\\ b_n\amp =\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi }^\pi f(t)\sin(nt)\dx{t}, \text{ if } n\ge 1. \end{align*}
