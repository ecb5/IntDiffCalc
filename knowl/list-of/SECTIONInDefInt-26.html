DIGRESSION: Leibniz and Huygens: Invention of Integration.

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus was known to both Newton and Leibniz, and some special cases were known to some of their predecessors. But Leibniz was the first to publish this result in the general form we see here. Leibniz’ written works on Calclulus are still very readable because the notation he used was so expressive that it is still in use today.
🔗
Gottfried Leibniz received his doctorate in jurisprudence from the University of Altdorf in 1667 at the age of 21 and was offered a professorship there but his intellectual interests were broad, and he perceived universities as places which tended to stifle creative thought, so he turned down the offer.
🔗
Leibniz was brilliant, but at the time his training in mathematics was rudimentary. While on a diplomatic mission to Paris in 1672 he met Christiann Huygens, one of the most brilliant mathematicians and pre-eminent scientists of the age and he persuaded Huygens to take him under his wing. Leibniz credited much of his mathematical growth in Paris to Huygens.
 4 
In Subsection 6.2.3 we saw how Huygens used the properties of the Brachistochrone to design a more accurate pendulum clock. His work on curvature is explored in the TRIUMPHS project The Radius of Curvature According to Christiaan Huygens by Jerry Lodder.
🔗
In order to assess the younger man’s abilities, Huygens posed the following problem as a challenge: Find the sum of the reciprocals of the triangular numbers. That is, he asked Leibniz to determine the sum
\begin{equation*} \frac{1}{1}+\frac{1}{1+2}+\frac{1}{1+2+3}+\frac{1}{1+2+3+4}+\cdots \end{equation*}
This problem had already been solved by Johann van Waveren Hudde whom we encountered in Problem 5.6.0.2, but Huygens was curious how the young Leibniz would approach it. Here is what he did.
🔗
Using the known formula \(1+2+3+\cdots +n=\frac{n\left(n+1\right)}{2}\text{,}\) he transformed the sum above to
\begin{equation*} \frac{2}{1\cdot 2}+\frac{2}{2\cdot 3}+\frac{2}{3\cdot 4}+\cdots\text{.} \end{equation*}
n Leibniz recognized that this could be rewritten as
\begin{equation*} 2\left[\left(\frac{1}{1} - \frac{1}{2}\right) + \left(\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{3}\right) + \left(\frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{4}\right) + \cdots \right] \end{equation*}
and noted that after subtraction only the first and last terms remain, leaving
\begin{equation*} 2\left(1-\frac{1}{\infty }\right)=2(1-0)=2. \end{equation*}
In the seventeenth century it was common to use the symbol “\(\infty \)” as if it were an actual number. But it is not. This usage is nonsense. Don’t do it. Your teacher will be horrified, possibly even angry.
🔗
It is not the result itself, which was already known, but the technique which is important here. Generalizing from this problem Leibniz surmised that the underlying idea—that the sum of differences is equal to the difference of the extremes—would apply equally well to infinitely small differences (differentials). He published this idea in a short paper in 1693 as follows.
🔗
If
\begin{equation*} \dfdx{A}{x}=y \end{equation*}
then
\begin{equation*} y\dx{x} =\dx{A} \text{.} \end{equation*}
Integrating we have
\begin{equation*} \int^b_{x=a}{y\dx{x} }=\int^b_{x=a}{\dx{A} }=A\left(b\right)-A(a)\text{.} \end{equation*}
🔗
We have used modern notation to highlight the association with definite integration. Leibniz’ explanation was much more wordy, and was accompanied by a very complicated diagram. Leibniz was fond of complicated diagrams. The diagram in Figure 20.3.0.7 below is a simplified version of Leibniz’ original.
🔗
Notice that since \(y\dx{x} =\dx{A}\) the numerical value of the area of the box with width \(\dx{x} \) and height \(y\) in the diagram on the left is the same as the numerical value of the length \(\dx{A} \) of the segment in the diagram on the right. If we add all of these together, the sum of the areas of the boxes in the diagram on the left will provide the area of the region; the sum of the lengths of the segments in the diagram on the right will provide the length of the segment from \(A(a)\) to \(A(b)\text{.}\)
🔗
In other words, we have
\begin{equation*} \int^b_{x=a}{y\dx{x} }=\int^b_{x=a}{\dx{A} }=A\left(b\right)-A(a) \end{equation*}
which is the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.
