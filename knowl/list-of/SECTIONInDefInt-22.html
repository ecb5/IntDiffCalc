The Definite Integral.

In part (c) of Example 20.3.0.2 we saw that we can compute the area under the graph of the function \({f(t)=t-\frac{t^2}{100}} \) between \(0\) and \(100\) by defining the accumulation function
\begin{equation*} F(x) =\int_{t=0}^{x}f(t)\dx{t} \end{equation*}
and evaluating it at \(x=100\text{.}\) Thus
\begin{equation*} F(100) =\int_{t=0}^{100}f(t)\dx{t}\text{.} \end{equation*}
🔗
We will often be interested in the value of our accumulation function at a single point only. In that case we will forego writing down the function \(F(x) \) and proceed immediately to evaluating \(\int_{t=a}^{b}f(t)\dx{t}\) where the lower index \(a\text{,}\) is where we begin accumulating the differentials and the upper index \(b\text{,}\) is where we stop. In that case since there is no confusion over the variable names, dummy or otherwise, we will usually revert to our lifelong habit of using \(x\) as the variable. Because it has a definite numerical value the integral \(\int_{t=a}^{b}f(x)\dx{x}\) is called a Definite Integral. The notation associated with integrals and antiderivatives very dense so it can be very confusing at first. A simple example will be helpful.
